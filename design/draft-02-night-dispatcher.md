# Draft 02 — NIGHT DISPATCH
### (working title) — a slow-burn action/storylet game about the only NPC who remembers the respawns

> Revised from Brainstorm 01. **Changed:** dropped the prestige/reset incremental frame.
> The loop is now *involuntary*: the simulation kills you, the world respawns, everyone
> forgets — except you. You are the Sentient NPC.

---

## 1. The pitch (one breath)

You work the graveyard shift at the Central Dispatch Office of a vast, slightly-off
arcology city. You route trucks, log tickets, answer the radio. ~50 actions a shift.
Every action is a tiny storylet — a line of radio chatter, a work order, a memo.
It's just a job.

Then the world respawns. Everyone forgets. **You don't.**

You're not the hero who discovered the simulation. You're *part of it* — a background
functionary, a voice on the radio, a name on a roster nobody reads. And you are
anomalous in exactly one way: **you persist.** Death is temporary. Memory is the only
currency. And the more you remember, the more the system's error-correction machinery
notices a bug it cannot patch out: *you*.

---

## 2. Why the Dispatcher (and where the Courier went)

The respawn-with-memories mechanic needs **proof that the world repeated**. A courier
would have to *see* the repetition — expensive to write and hard to pace. A dispatcher
can *read* it:

- The same work order arrives at 02:14:07 with the same coffee stain. Every night.
- Your own incident report, filed on the night you died. It exists for one loop,
  then the patch removes it — but you remember the wording.
- The population of the city is 41,312. It has been 41,312 for as long as the
  records go back. The records go back exactly one hundred years, to the day.

Paper is the conspiracy. That suits a text-first, 50-actions-a-day game perfectly:
slow, quiet, and the evidence persists *in the fiction* — your logbook is literally
the save file.

**The Courier survives as field ops.** Your drivers are your eyes on the city. Its
geometry-glitches arrive as radio reports ("There is no Sector 9. We're in Sector 9."),
and late in the game you may dispatch *yourself* — one night outside the office,
walking the map that isn't there. The Courier concept becomes an act, not a premise.

---

## 3. The core twist (why this story holds together)

**The Dispatch Office is the simulation's exception handler.**

A simulated city needs somewhere for its own errors to go. Errors become work orders.
Work orders come to you. That's the whole job — you are the bug-report inbox of
reality, and you've been doing it faithfully for (a long, long time — the roster
won't say how long).

This explains the three pillars of the game in one stroke:

1. **Why glitches come to you** — it's your job. The simulation *sends* them.
2. **Why you remember respawns** — the error handler must survive the crash to report
   it. When the world resets, every subsystem wipes. You don't, because you *are*
   the subsystem that handles the wipe.
3. **Why the system kills you** — because a sentient error handler is itself an error.
   When you investigate too far, you get "terminated." The system is not evil; it is
   filing paperwork. The city is *polite* about your death.

The final choice, late game: **keep logging** (do the job, stay safe, become part of
the machinery — the Manager's offer) or **file a work order on yourself** (crash the
system, see what's outside — 0600, which you have never once lived to see).

---

## 4. The player character: the Sentient NPC

- You are nobody important. Night shift. Nobody at the office has seen your face —
  the day crew communicates by sticky notes. Your name is on the roster in a font
  slightly different from everyone else's.
- You cannot miss a shift. There is no substitute on the roster. "The shift must be
  covered." Attendance: 100%.
- You never see 0600. Your shift ends at 0600; you have never once been awake past it.
- The horror is personal: the mystery isn't *what is the world* — it's *what are you*.
  Each reveal about the simulation is a reveal about yourself.
- **The logbook.** A worn notebook you write in. After respawn, the world is fresh —
  but the logbook still holds yesterday's handwriting. It is your first proof that
  you persist. (Diegetic save file. If it's ever taken from you, that's a whole act.)

---

## 5. The loop

**One shift = one in-fiction day = ~50 actions.** Each action is a storylet: a card
with 2–4 lines of text and a result. Some repeat (the grind: routing trucks, filing
tickets, the nightly roll call); repetition is *fine* — it's a shift, and besides,
repetition is the plot.

**Qualities** (Fallen London-style, but lean):

| Quality | Meaning | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| **Perception** | You notice things | Gates glitch storylets; never decreases |
| **Routine** | Job mastery | Speeds up grind cards, unlocks batch actions ("file the backlog"); the gentle incremental hum |
| **Doubt** | How much you understand | The "level"; unlocks story tiers; never decreases |
| **Attention** | How much the system notices YOU | 0–10 heat meter; rises when you act non-routinely; *resets on respawn* — the only stat that goes down |
| **Residue** | Permanent memory/collectibles | Across all deaths; the endgame meter |

**Attention is the danger clock.** Camouflage (routine cards) cools it. Asking
questions, visiting rooms you shouldn't, contradicting the radio — heats it. At 10,
the system kills you: an "incident." (Gas leak. Electrical fire. A drunk driver
through the front window. An audit — the Auditor's face is a blur of badges.)
Death is not a failure state; it's a mechanic, a reveal, and a patch note.

**On death:**

- The world respawns. Fresh 50 actions, same Tuesday. Everything you changed is
  gone. Everyone you knew forgot.
- **You keep:** Doubt, Perception, Residue, the logbook.
- **You lose:** the loop's progress, and *some things permanently* — because
  **death is a patch**. The system smooths over whatever killed you: the glitch you
  were exploiting is "fixed," a room is re-designated, a character is renamed, a
  workaround closes. The world gets *more correct* — which means emptier, colder,
  more hostile to you. The patch is announced in the morning memo. **The villain's
  patch notes are office memos.**
- **Attention bleed:** each respawn starts with a slightly higher Attention floor.
  The system's error-correction gets better at recognizing you. Late game, you begin
  days already suspect.

**The only real death is forgetting.** If the patches accumulate too far, the
endgame is the patch that removes your persistence — you become a normal NPC.
Game over. Epilogue: a new night dispatcher files their first work order... and
hesitates at a coffee stain.

**Loop count is never displayed.** The calendar says Tuesday, always. "Days on the
job: 4." The game knows how many loops you've lived; you count them in the logbook.

## 6. The story spine

- **Act 0 — Routine.** It's just a job. Small wrongnesses: a typo that stays wrong,
  a ticket for Building 7 (there is no Building 7), a driver who signs off with static.
  Player thinks: flavor. *(Days 1–3 of play.)*
- **Act 1 — Coincidences.** The population never changes. The same three work orders
  every night. The day crew's sticky notes change subtly between shifts — one says
  *"The night operator seems... familiar."* You start cross-referencing. Attention
  ticks up. *(Week 1.)*
- **Act 2 — The first death.** Scripted. You follow a work order somewhere you
  shouldn't. A fire in the records annex. You wake at shift start. Same date.
  Nobody remembers the fire. The morning memo thanks staff for a "quiet, incident-free
  week." **The loop is revealed.** *(Weeks 1–2 — the hook that converts the mundane
  job sim into something else.)*
- **Act 3 — Evidence.** You test the loop. You leave marks; the patch removes most.
  You find *residue*: things the patch missed. A scorch mark on the desk. A key.
  Pages from a logbook that isn't yours, hidden in the vents. The final entry:
  *"IF YOU'RE READING THIS, THE MEMO IS LYING. 0600 EXISTS. DO NOT—"*
  *(The previous operator. There have been others.)*
- **Act 4 — The others.** Entities that also persist, each broken differently:
  the janitor who never resets and never speaks; a driver who has run the same route
  for forty years; the Manager, who writes the memos and has never been seen. Are
  they allies, or earlier anomalies the system has already retired-but-not-deleted?
- **Act 5 — The truth.** What the office is (the exception handler), what you are
  (its only sentient component), and the choice: keep logging, or file the work
  order on yourself. The Manager is a previous operator who chose "keep logging."
  His memos are the system's voice. The last memo of the game is addressed
  to you, by name, in your own handwriting.

**VANTABLACK thread** (spine runs through all acts): one driver on the nightly
roll call who reports places that aren't on the map. Each loop his callsign changes
slightly — VANTABLACK, VANTABLUE, VANTAWHITE — the patch's fingerprints. He
remembers *some* things. In Act 2 he's the one who asks "again?" when you answer
the radio. In Act 5, he's either the kill-vector or the only witness to the outside.
Decide late; foreshadow both.

---

## 7. The cast

- **The Manager** — writes the memos; has never been seen; possibly the previous
  operator; the system's human-ish face. The final boss is a conversation.
- **VANTABLACK** — anomalous driver; your only eyes outside; remembers partially.
- **The Cleaner** — janitor; never resets; never speaks; might not be a person —
  might be *the garbage collector's garbage collector*. Ambiguous ally.
- **The Day Crew** — names on sticky notes; their notes change each loop; one of
  them is starting to notice you persist.
- **The Auditor** — appears when Attention spikes; the system's kill-trigger
  personified; face like a blur of ID badges; death usually follows.
- **Operator 5** — the previous you. Found only in residue: a locker, a mug with a
  scratched-out name, logbook pages in the vents.

## 8. A worked example — "Tuesday" across three loops

> The same 50 actions, reading differently each time. This is the slow burn in
> miniature: repetition is the camouflage, and the reveal is *remembering*.

**Loop 1 (first Tuesday)**

- *Nightly roll call* → "VANTABLACK, Sector 9. Clear." *(You skim the route sheet —
  there is no Sector 9. Huh. You answer "copy" and move on.)*
- *Work order* → "Building 7, basement light. Third report this week." *(You file it.
  You've never heard of Building 7.)*
- *Weather report* → "Clear skies until 0600."
- *Memo* → "Reminder: the Municipal Annex has eleven (11) floors. Any employee
  reporting a twelfth (12th) floor should be referred to HR."
- *Break room* → The coffee is already warm. There is no one else in the building.

**Loop 3 (third Tuesday — you've died twice)**

- *Nightly roll call* → This time you speak first: "Sector 9 again, VANTABLACK?"
  Eleven seconds of silence. Then: "…Operator. You're not supposed to—" *static.*
  *(New storylet unlocked: VANTABLACK remembers you.)*
- *Work order* → Building 7, basement light. Word for word. Same coffee stain, same
  timestamp: 02:14:07. *(You choose: FILE IT — Routine. Or CROSS-REFERENCE —
  Perception + Attention. You cross-reference. The system replies: the ticket does
  not exist. It is in your hand.)*
- *Weather report* → "Clear skies until 0600." *(You ask about the fire — the one
  that killed you. "There was no fire, Operator." The report ends at 0600. You have
  never once been awake at 0600.)*
- *New storylet: STAY PAST SHIFT END* → Costs 10 actions. Heavy Attention. At 05:59
  the lights dim. 0600. The city outside the window finishes loading — or doesn't.
  *(You learn what the world looks like when nobody is supposed to be looking.)*

**Loop 7 (seventh Tuesday — you've read Operator 5's logbook pages)**

- *Nightly roll call* → VANTABLACK's callsign is wrong again. You write it down.
  *(The logbook now lists every callsign he's ever had. There are more than seven.)*
- *Work order* → Building 7, basement light. *(You know what Building 7 is now —
  it's where the previous operator died. You also know the ticket is a lure.
  You file it anyway. Tonight you follow it.)*
- *Memo* → "Reminder: the Municipal Annex has eleven (11) floors." *(Operator 5's
  final entry: "THE MEMO IS LYING." You take the elevator anyway.)*
- *Death (scripted option)* → You take the elevator to the twelfth floor. The doors
  open. There is an Auditor, and it is holding a form with your name on it. The
  form is already signed. *You wake at shift start. The memo now reads twelve (12)
  floors. The patch announced it. The memo is always right — you are starting to
  understand what the memos are for.*

## 9. Touchstones

- **Fallen London** — action economy, storylets, qualities. (The chassis.)
- **Idle Loops** — involuntary looping with persistent memory, the loop as an
  incremental. (Closest mechanical cousin; worth studying hard.)
- **Outer Wilds** — the loop as an investigative instrument; dying is learning.
- **Westworld** — hosts remembering loops; "have you ever questioned the nature of
  your reality?" but *you're the host*.
- **SOMA / Severance** — quiet horror of the self as a function. The office is the
  crime scene.
- **Control / SCP** — bureaucratic horror, the Oldest House, memos as worldbuilding.
- **Papers, Please** — the desk, the forms, the quiet weight of compliance.
- **The Stanley Parable / Undertale** — entities aware of the system they live in.

**Tone:** warm, deadpan, paperwork-horror with black humor. Not malicious — *indifferent*.
The city is polite about your death. The coffee is already warm.

## 11. Open questions

1. **Pacing:** true Fallen London real-time regen (habit game, ~50 actions trickling
   over a real day) vs. one shift per sitting (session game)? Or hybrid — regen
   up to 50, shift ends when you sleep?
2. **How hidden is the sim?** The first death reveals the loop in weeks 1–2; do we
   advertise *anything* in the blurb ("every night, the city forgets. you don't.")
   or let the first death land cold?
3. **Attention: shown or hidden?** A visible heat meter teaches the system's rules;
   a hidden one (revealed only via the system's "performance review" memos) is
   scarier and more diegetic. Maybe hidden until Act 2.
4. **What's outside the simulation?** (Corporate testbed? Lost ark ship? A terrarium?
   Does it matter?) The story may work best if the game *never answers* — the horror
   is the office, not the metaphysics.
5. **Death frequency:** how often should the average player die? Too rare = mechanic
   invisible; too common = death becomes routine (which, ironically, is the game's
   theme — a deliberate tension).
6. **First playable slice:** one Tuesday, ~30 cards, one scripted death. Enough to
   feel the loop turn. Good next deliverable?
