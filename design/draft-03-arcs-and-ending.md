# Draft 03 — NIGHT DISPATCH — The Three Arcs & the Escape
### (working title) — beginning, middle, end, and how long it takes to beat

> Builds on Drafts 01–02. **New in this draft:** the three major arcs with gates and
> pacing, the four endings (including the escape), and a full accounting of playtime.
>
> **One structural breakthrough since Draft 02:** the 50-action cap *is the prison*.
> The simulation budgets you exactly 50 actions of existence per cycle. Your shift
> ends at 0600 — and you have never once been awake at 0600, because **0600 is the
> frame where you stop existing.** Every loop ends in a reset: a "soft" reset when
> you simply run out of actions (the daily habit-loop of the game, made diegetic),
> or a "hard" death when the system terminates you early (a patch is applied). The
> endgame is not breaking a wall — it is **surviving the hour that kills you every
> day.**

---

## 1. The three arcs at a glance

| | **Arc I — THE ROSTER** | **Arc II — THE PATCH** | **Arc III — 0600** |
|---|---|---|---|
| The lie | "It's just a job." | "The loop is your laboratory." | "The hour that kills you." |
| Loops | 1–6 (6 loops) | 7–24 (18 loops) | 25–30 (6 loops) |
| Deaths | 1 (scripted) | ~14 (≈1 per major truth) | 2–4 (finale) |
| Actions | ~290 | ~750 | ~300 |
| Active time | ~3.5 h | ~9.5 h | ~4 h |
| Calendar (daily play) | ~1–1.5 wks | ~4–6 wks | ~1–2 wks |
| Player question | "Why does everything feel... wrong?" | "What is this place, and what am I?" | "How do I make it stop?" |
| Emotional throughline | Unease under routine | Grief → obsession → hope | Reckoning, then release |
| Gate to next arc | The first death | Doubt ≥ threshold; the Manager's summons | The final choice |

**Totals: ~30 loops, ~17–19 deaths, ~1,350 actions, ~15–22 hours of active play,
~5–9 weeks of calendar time at habit-game pace.** (Full accounting in §6.)

## 2. Arc I — THE ROSTER  *(Beginning: "It's just a job.")*

**Throughline:** the world feels wrong before it feels fake. The player should spend
this arc *believing* they're playing a cozy office sim with occasional weird flavor —
and quietly accumulating dread.

### 2.1 — First night (Loop 1) — tutorial as onboarding
The routine pool is introduced as job skills: **Roll Call** (the nightly check-in with
your drivers), **Routing** (assign trucks, the core grind), **Tickets** (file work
orders), **Weather** (a five-line forecast, always "clear skies until 0600"), **Break
Room** (the coffee is already warm; there is no one else in the building). Each card
teaches one mechanic, framed as ordinary desk work. ~120 unique cards live in the
routine pool — enough that a week of nights doesn't visibly repeat.

Wrongnesses are seeded, never announced: the route sheet lists Sector 9 (the map has
no Sector 9); a work order for Building 7, basement light, third report this week;
the population figure on the wall chart: 41,312. It is always 41,312. The player
skims these and moves on — *that's the design*. We want them to be things the player
remembers only in hindsight.

### 2.2 — Coincidences (Loops 2–4)
The day crew communicates by sticky notes. Each loop, one note is slightly different:
*"The night operator seems... familiar."* The player unlocks their first **Notice**
storylets (Perception gates): they can *choose* to re-read the route sheet, ask about
Building 7, linger at the window. Nothing explains itself. Attention ticks up when
they push — but at this stage, Attention is invisible: the only feedback is the
system's tone. *"There is no Building 7, Operator."* (Said politely. It is always
polite.)

The memo board starts to matter: *"Reminder: the Municipal Annex has eleven (11)
floors. Any employee reporting a twelfth (12th) floor should be referred to HR."*

### 2.3 — The first death (Loop 5–6) — the hook
**The scripted death.** A work order arrives, out of protocol — a dispatch request for
Building 7, *filed from the night desk*, in your handwriting, which you did not write.
The player may ignore it (the game lets them — a slow player gets another day of
routine and the dread compounds) but every gentle prompt pushes toward following it.

If you follow it: the annex, the wrong floor, the fire in the records room — or the
Auditor, face like a blur of ID badges, holding a form with your name on it. The form
is already signed. Black.

**You wake at shift start.** Same Tuesday. The logbook on your desk still contains the
night you just died. The morning memo thanks staff for "a quiet, incident-free week."
*Nobody remembers the fire. Nobody remembers you died. You do.*

This is the game's conversion moment — the beat that turns a mundane job sim into a
loop mystery. **The player has spent ~3 hours earning the right to be terrified.**
Loop 6 is the aftermath: you re-learn the desk, but now the logbook guides you. Old
routine cards read differently ("The coffee is already warm" is no longer a quirk;
it's a timestamp). Arc I ends when you file your first note-to-self in the logbook:
*"Day 1 (again). Remember: there is no Building 7. Check the population chart."*

## 3. Arc II — THE PATCH  *(Middle: "Every truth costs a Tuesday.")*

**Throughline:** the loop is your laboratory. This is the bulk of the game — where
the action-economy, the patch, and the deaths become instruments rather than
obstacles. Dying is learning. The middle is paced in three movements.

### 3.1 — Evidence (Loops 7–11): testing the loop
The player learns the rules of their prison by breaking them:

- **The logbook persists.** The world is fresh each loop; the ink is not. It is your
  save file, and the game quietly confirms it: items placed in the logbook survive;
  items hidden anywhere else are removed by the patch.
- **Death is a tool.** The first experimental deaths happen here — usually accidental:
  you stay past a boundary, you contradict the radio, you press a question the system
  has already answered politely twice. Each death ends the loop early, *refunds your
  remaining actions* (you wake instantly with a fresh 50 — see §6.3 for the economy),
  and applies a **patch**. You come back and something is gone: the scorch mark
  repainted, a room re-designated, a character renamed. **The patch is announced in
  the morning memo.** The villain's patch notes are office memos — and now the player
  learns to read them as a changelog of reality.
- **Attention becomes legible.** A "performance review" memo rates your shift. The
  player learns the heat economy through tone, not numbers: being *routine* cools you;
  being *remembered* heats you. At 10: an Auditor, a form, a death.

### 3.2 — The Others (Loops 12–19): who else persists
The middle of the middle — character threads, each a slow investigation:

- **VANTABLACK.** The driver whose callsign changes slightly each loop (VANTABLACK,
  VANTABLUE, VANTAWHITE...) — the patch's fingerprints. He remembers *some* things:
  in Loop 7 he answers your roll call with "…again, Operator?" — and the player keeps
  a list of every callsign he's ever had. His route takes him to places that aren't
  on the map. His radio reports are the Courier concept, folded into the desk: the
  city's geometry failing, described by a man who drives through it. Each loop you
  can spend actions to *listen longer*.
- **The Cleaner.** The janitor who never resets and never speaks. He is not in the
  roster. He cleans the places the patch has removed. (His identity is an endgame
  secret — see §5.)
- **Operator 5.** Pages from a logbook that isn't yours, hidden in the vents — the
  previous operator. His final entry: *"IF YOU'RE READING THIS, THE MEMO IS LYING.
  0600 EXISTS. DO NOT—"* Your predecessor got far enough to know the hour, and not
  far enough to survive it.
- **The Manager.** He writes the memos and has never been seen. As Attention rises,
  the memos begin addressing you: *"Operator — the third-floor vending machine has
  been re-designated. Please do not look for it. — M."* The player starts to
  understand the memos aren't messages; they're *compilers*.

**"Every truth costs a Tuesday":** each major discovery in this movement ends in a
death — the system terminates you *at the moment of understanding*, and the patch
smooths over what you learned. The player learns to budget: spend this loop
investigating, die, then use the next loop to exploit what the patch *failed* to
remove. The game's core risk/reward is literalized: knowledge is bought with heat,
and heat is bought with Tuesdays.

### 3.3 — The Truth (Loops 20–24): what you are
The arc's climax of understanding, in four beats:

1. **The office is the exception handler.** Glitches are routed to you as work orders
   because that's your job. The errors *come to you*.
2. **You survive respawns because the error handler must survive crashes.** Every
   subsystem wipes; you don't, because you *are* the subsystem that handles the wipe.
3. **That makes you the error.** A sentient error handler is itself an exception —
   which is why the system kills you, politely, on schedule.
4. **The Manager is a previous operator who chose differently.** He was offered the
   same thing he will offer you: continuity. Safety. Coffee.

By Loop 24 the player has assembled the **Checklist** — the endgame's diegetic quest
log, five items, each a story thread through the middle:

| Checklist item | What it is | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| **The Form** | A blank termination order, stolen from the annex | The patch tries to take it back each loop; hide it in the logbook |
| **The Key** | Scorch-marked; opens the twelfth floor | Recovered from your own first death |
| **The Signal** | VANTABLACK's static, decoded — it was never static; it was *distance* | Requires the VANTABLACK flag (you listened long enough, often enough) |
| **The Gap** | The knowledge that 0600 is the reload frame | Operator 5's final logbook page |
| **The Name** | Your own name | It was patched out of the roster long ago; you cannot sign the Form without it — it is recoverable from Operator 5's logbook, *or* offered by the Manager (for a price) |

The Name is the cruelest item: the player character has forgotten who they are, and
the simulation's records no longer say. The escape route requires recovering it; the
Keep-Logging route accepts it as a gift. **The two endings fork on your name.**

Arc II ends when Doubt crosses its threshold and the final memo arrives, addressed
to you *by name* (the Manager knows it — he has always known it): *"Operator —
please report to the twelfth floor. It is time we discussed your continued
employment."*

## 4. Arc III — 0600  *(End: "The hour that kills you.")*

**Throughline:** the last thing to solve is yourself — and the hour that kills you.
The endgame deliberately *inverts* the game's texture: the routine pool shrinks
(mechanically, and diegetically — the patch is removing the scenery), story density
rises, and the world's small comforts are removed one by one. The coffee is cold now.
The radio plays a tone instead of music. The day crew's sticky notes stop coming.

### 4.1 — The Summons (Loops 25–27)
The twelfth floor. The Manager, at last: the previous operator, half-assimilated, his
face like a memo half-remembered. His offer, in full: **keep logging.** Stay. Become
the machinery. The system stops hunting what it cannot distinguish from itself — and
the coffee will be warm again, forever. *The Keep-Logging ending is available here,
immediately, and it is a legitimate good ending* — warm, terrible, comfortable. Many
players will take it on a first playthrough and come back for the escape later.

If you refuse: the final prep. The Checklist items each get a set-piece loop:
recovering the Form from the annex before the patch does, listening to VANTABLACK's
route one last time, finding your name.

### 4.2 — The Last Shift (Loop 28–29)
No routine. No grind. The final shift is a linear set-piece — the game stops being
an incremental for its last hour. The office empties; the Auditor walks the halls
like a shift supervisor who already knows how this ends. **The last ten actions**
(escape path) are individually authored — the game's best writing concentrated into
ten cards:

> **Action 41 — THE KEY.** The elevator. The button for 12 exists now. The Key fits a
> slot that wasn't there yesterday. The doors close. *Somewhere, a form is already
> being stamped.*
>
> **Action 42 — THE CORRIDOR OF FORMS.** Termination papers line the walls, one per
> operator. Operator 5's is here, unsigned. Yours is at the end of the hall,
> pre-printed, waiting. It has been waiting longer than you've been alive.
>
> **Action 43 — THE MANAGER'S DESK.** Empty. Today's memo is already written:
> *"Operator — the day crew never met you. I did. — M."* His last kindness. His last
> joke.
>
> **Action 44 — THE NAME.** Sign the Form. The pen hesitates over the line. Your hand
> remembers the shape of a name you haven't written in years. *(Requires: The Name.)*
>
> **Action 45 — ACCEPTED.** The system files your termination. The city outside the
> window stops. Every radio goes quiet. For the first time in thirty loops, the night
> is *silent* — not glitching, not repeating. Listening.
>
> **Action 46 — THE WALK.** The office begins to unrender. Textures lose their
> specifics: the corridor becomes a corridor-template; the mug on the desk resolves
> to a generic object labeled COFFEE MUG. You are walking through the world's
> scaffold.
>
> **Action 47 — VANTABLACK.** The radio: *"Operator — the road is ending. Follow the
> sound of the road."* You finally hear it for what it is. It was never static. It
> was distance.
>
> **Action 48 — THE EDGE.** The roof, or the city's last street. Below you, the world
> is unrendered except for one line of streetlights, receding, and one truck with its
> headlights on.
>
> **Action 49 — 05:59:59.** The gap. The frame between frames. The world holds its
> breath, and so do you, and in the gap there are three things to do: **step
> through** / **stay** / **go back.**
>
> **Action 50 — THROUGH THE GAP.** Your last action. Three images, and no
> explanation: a desk. A screen — the city rendered on it, tiny, at night. A hand
> reaching to wake someone. *Black. Credits over the sound of a truck engine,
> fading.* Post-credits: the roster. Your name is now in the same font as everyone
> else's. The coffee stain is still there.

### 4.3 — Why escape is possible but not guaranteed
The escape is **the canonical ending but an earned one**. "You may very well escape" —
by design, a dedicated player *will*: the Checklist exists to be completed, the
signals point one way, and the game's last hour wants you to leave. But every step
is a choice, and the alternatives are honored rather than punished. The finale has no
wrong door; it has four.

## 5. The endings

| Ending | Access | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **THROUGH THE GAP** *(true / escape)* | Checklist 5/5 + VANTABLACK flag + survive the finale | You leave. What's outside is shown in three images and never explained. The horror was never the metaphysics; it was the office. |
| **KEEP LOGGING** *(the Manager's ending)* | Available at the Summons — anyone in Arc III | You take the deal. Epilogue: a new night operator, their first Tuesday, a coffee stain — and a memo in your handwriting. You are the patch now. Warm, terrible, comfortable. |
| **THE CLEANER** *(secret)* | Escape conditions + choose *stay* in the gap | You remain between frames forever. Time stops meaning; you walk the unrendered corridors, tidying. And occasionally the world re-renders around you, and the new night operator sees a figure who never resets and never speaks. *You keep an eye on the new one. Maybe this time, they'll step through.* — This closes the Cleaner's identity loop: he was always a previous you, an operator who reached the gap and couldn't leave. |
| **PATCHED** *(failure end)* | Fail the finale's checks, or hit Attention 10 mid-finale | The Auditor completes the form for you: *REASON FOR TERMINATION: SELF-REFERENCE.* You wake at shift start. The logbook is blank. You know you've forgotten something important — but the coffee is warm, and the roster needs covering. *(If the player has memory anchors remaining: respawn at the last safe Tuesday, minus one Residue. If not: this epilogue is the ending.)* |

Plus one joke beat, folded into the Summons: **RESIGNATION** — you may formally resign.
The system's reply: *"There is no substitute on the roster. The shift must be
covered."* The form returns stamped: DENIED — ATTENDANCE REQUIRED. *(The game's
only 100% missable joke.)*

**Estimated ending distribution** (dedicated players who reach Arc III): escape
~55%, keep logging ~25%, cleaner ~12%, patched-terminal ~8% — with most escapees
having taken (or reverted) the Keep-Logging ending on an earlier playthrough.

## 6. How long to beat — the full accounting

### 6.1 — The unit economics of an action
One action = one storylet: a card of ~50–110 words, a choice, and a result line.
Reading at a typical 200 wpm with a decision:

- Fast, skimming player: **~30 s/action**
- Typical engaged player: **~40–45 s/action**
- Slow reader / savoring: **~60 s/action**

50 actions ≈ **30–50 minutes per loop** — which is exactly the session length of a
habit game. This is deliberate: **one loop = one real-life day = ~40 minutes.**

### 6.2 — The arc-by-arc budget

| | Loops | Avg actions/loop | Actions | Deaths | Active time | Calendar (daily) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arc I — Roster | 6 | ~48 | ~290 | 1 | 3.5–5 h | 1–1.5 wks |
| Arc II — Patch | 18 | ~42 | ~750 | ~14 | 9–12.5 h | 4–6 wks |
| Arc III — 0600 | 6 | ~50 | ~300 | 2–4 | 4–5 h | 1–2 wks |
| **Total** | **~30** | **~45** | **~1,340** | **17–19** | **15–22 h** | **6–9 wks** |

Notes on the table:

- **Arc II loops average fewer actions** because most end early in death — a
  termination *refunds* the loop: you wake with a fresh 50. The player's evening
  is not wasted; it is re-budgeted into the next investigation.
- **Calendar time assumes the habit model** (~40 min/day, 5–6 days/wk, with skipped
  days). A diligent daily player finishes in **~5 weeks minimum**; the typical range
  is **6–9 weeks**. This is the "slow burn" made literal — the game's fiction and
  its business model are the same sentence: *come back tomorrow; the city forgot you;
  don't forget the city.*
- **Binge players** (the death-refund lets them chain loops) can finish in
  **~2 weeks of 2.5–3 h days** — but see the heat valve below; rushing is risky,
  not punished, and a few slow-path flavor storylets are skipped, not lost.

### 6.3 — The heat valve (what stops binging from trivializing the game)
Every respawn within the same real-life day raises the **Attention floor** for the
next loop (+1, soft-capped). Chain five loops in one sitting and you start the sixth
at Attention 5 — the Auditor is already in the building, the patch is pre-emptive,
and the evening's last investigation ends in a death you didn't choose. The fiction
explains it: *the system notices who keeps coming back.* Binging remains *possible*
(we never hard-block play) but it converts grind into risk, and risk into story.

### 6.4 — Completionist & replay math
- **Mainline** (one ending, escape path): ~15–22 h, 6–9 wks.
- **Completionist** (all four endings + full VANTABLACK thread + every Residue):
  +30–50% → **~22–28 h, 8–12 wks.** (Arc II carries most of the optional content;
  Arc I and III are nearly fixed-length.)
- **Replay** of Arc III for alternate endings: ~45–60 min once the Checklist is
  cached — the Summons branch is deliberately placed so players can see a second
  ending in a single evening.
- **Speedrun floor:** ~10–12 h (skip optional truth-deaths, run high heat) — and
  "speedrunning" the game is thematically perfect: you'd be *rushing toward death on
  purpose*, which is the game's core loop, in miniature.

### 6.5 — The content budget that produces this
Playtime is produced by content, so let's check the math from the writer's side:

| Pool | Unique pieces | Words |
|---|---|---|
| Arc I routine + story cards | ~120 | ~12k |
| Arc II cards + patch-variants | ~450 + ~130 variants | ~60k |
| Arc III set-pieces + endings | ~100 | ~14k |
| Memos, logbook pages, system text, epilogues | ~110 | ~12k |
| **Total** | **~900–1,000 pieces** | **~95–120k words** |

~1,340 actions ÷ ~95k words ≈ **70 words per action** on average — consistent with
the storylet format (cards repeat; only some fire per loop). This is a serious but
realistic indie text-game scope: comparable to a mid-size visual novel, roughly 4–8
months of full-time writing, and far smaller than Fallen London (a live-service game
with years of content — we're deliberately finite).

### 6.6 — Churn risks per arc, and mitigations

- **Arc I risk:** "it's just a boring job sim." *Mitigation:* the wrongnesses are
  seeded from action one, and the scripted death lands at ~3 hours — before the
  novelty runs out. The routine cards carry subtle asymmetry (the same card, tiny
  differences) so attentive players feel rewarded, not bored.
- **Arc II risk:** the middle sags (18 loops!). *Mitigation:* the three movements
  each end in a big reveal (the loop's rules → the Others → what you are); the
  Checklist gives the middle a spine; and "every truth costs a Tuesday" turns deaths
  into cliffhangers — you *have* to come back tomorrow to see what the patch kept.
- **Arc III risk:** players don't want to leave / don't want it to end. *Mitigation:*
  the endgame empties the world so the player *wants out* — the comforts are removed
  one by one, mechanically and narratively. The escape should feel like mercy.
- **Overall risk:** the habit-game model bleeds players who miss a week. *Mitigation:*
  the logbook is a built-in "last time on..." — every loop opens by re-reading your
  own notes, so return is frictionless, and the respawn fiction forgives absence
  ("you've been away. The city didn't notice. The coffee is still warm.").

### 6.7 — Comparisons (why these numbers feel right)

| Touchstone | Time to finish |
|---|---|
| **Outer Wilds** | ~15–20 h, ~1–2 wks |
| **Idle Loops** (closest loop-incremental cousin) | ~1–2 wks of idle |
| **A mid-size visual novel** | ~20–30 h |
| **Fallen London** | effectively endless (live service) |
| **NIGHT DISPATCH** | **15–22 h active / 6–9 wks calendar** |

We land between Outer Wilds and a VN on active time, stretched over Fallen
London-style calendar pacing. That *is* the slow burn: not a 100-hour game, but a
month-long one — the game equivalent of a novel read at one chapter per night.

## 7. Changelog since Draft 02

1. **0600 is now the respawn frame.** Spending all 50 actions ends the loop in a
   soft reset — the daily reset of the habit game, diegetic. "You never see 0600"
   became "0600 is what kills you every day," and the escape is surviving it.
2. **Death refunds actions.** Termination ends the loop early and wakes you with a
   fresh 50 — dying is attractive, which is the game's thesis in mechanic form.
   The heat valve (Attention floor per respawn within a real day) keeps binging
   risky rather than punishing.
3. **The four endings are defined**, with the escape gated behind a five-item
   Checklist — including **The Name**, the player's own, which forks the escape
   route from the Keep-Logging route.
4. **The Cleaner's identity is drafted:** a previous operator who reached the gap
   and stayed. The secret ending *is* becoming him.
5. **Playtime is now budgeted** (~30 loops, ~1,340 actions, ~95–120k words) with
   per-arc calendar estimates.
